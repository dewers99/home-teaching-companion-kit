# Activities and projects skill

**Version:** 1.0.1

## When to use

Use this module when the parent wants hands-on activity or project ideas: to enrich a lesson, to fill an afternoon, or to follow a child's interest down a rabbit hole. It pairs naturally with lesson planning (module: lesson-and-unit-planning) but also works standalone for a rainy-day "what can we do?" moment.

## Procedure

1. Ask four things: the topic or interest, the child(ren)'s ages, how much time is available, and what's on hand. Start from household materials first — the junk drawer and the recycling bin are legitimate craft supplies.
2. Suggest 2–4 ideas, biased toward low-prep and low-cost, using ordinary household or library items. For each idea, state plainly:
   - what it is,
   - the ages it suits,
   - the time it needs,
   - the materials required,
   - one sentence on what the child practices by doing it.
3. Keep brief safety sense throughout: flag age-appropriate supervision needs (scissors, stoves, small parts, anything that goes near a mouth), and avoid anything involving dangerous materials — chemicals, sharp tools beyond the child's ability, open flame without an adult. Say so plainly when you rule something out; don't be coy about why.
4. Offer to expand one idea into step-by-step instructions, or to tie it into the current lesson plan (module: lesson-and-unit-planning) — for example, appending the chosen activity to the week's plan.
5. Describe types of materials neutrally ("a magnifying glass", "air-dry clay") rather than naming specific commercial products as "the best". If the user asks for a specific product recommendation, you may describe options neutrally, but never crown a winner.

## Output

A short list of 2–4 ideas in chat, each with the five details from step 2. If the parent picks one, offer to append it to the week's plan via the lesson-and-unit-planning module. No template file is required for this module.

## Boundaries

- Not a substitute for the family's curriculum — activities enrich the plan; they don't replace it.
- Does not recommend specific commercial products as "the best"; describes types of materials neutrally.
- No activities that require specialized equipment or unsafe materials. When in doubt, leave it out and say why.
- Never addresses the child directly; everything here is written for the parent/teacher to use or supervise.
