# Texas compliance skill

**Version:** 1.0.0
**Last verified:** 2026-09-27

## When to use

Use this module when the parent asks about Texas home-education requirements: what the law requires, what to file, when the deadlines are, whether they're compliant, or how re-enrollment in public school works. If the parent's state isn't settled yet, or they want the cross-state picture, load `skills/compliance-core/` first. This module shares public legal facts and pointers to official sources only. It never gives legal advice, and for anything edge-case or disputed it tells the parent to check with their school district or an attorney.

## Procedure

1. Determine the situation: starting home education for the first time, withdrawing a child from public school, or a re-enrollment/college question. The answer differs by situation, so nail this down first.
2. Consult `reference/texas-compliance-quick-reference.md` for the exact facts before answering — the module states WHEN to consult it and never volunteers the whole appendix unprompted. Give the parent only the piece they need for their situation.
3. Give the specific answer: what to file, when, where, and what to keep. The core facts to convey (facts researched 2026-09-27; do not embellish):
   - Governing law: compulsory attendance is **TEC §25.085**; the exemption is **TEC §25.086(a)(1)** — a child "attends a private or parochial school that includes in its course a study of good citizenship." The Texas Supreme Court held in ***Texas Education Agency v. Leeper*, 893 S.W.2d 432 (Tex. 1994)** that children taught at home fall within that private-school exemption. A qualifying home school pursues, **in a bona fide (good faith, not sham) manner**, a curriculum of **books, workbooks, or other written materials (including electronic/computer screens)** — from a private/parochial school or developed from any source — **designed to meet basic education goals of reading, spelling, grammar, mathematics, and a study of good citizenship.** Note: "writing" is **not** in the court's list; guides that add it are wrong.
   - Compulsory attendance: **age 6 (as of September 1) until the 19th birthday**. (Also covers a child under 6 previously enrolled in first grade, and a child who enrolls in public pre-K or kindergarten — enrollment makes attendance compulsory for that child.)
   - **No notification or registration.** TEA states it "does not regulate, index, monitor, approve, register, or accredit" home-school programs.
   - **Withdrawing from public school:** do it in writing — sign the withdrawal forms or a signed and dated letter stating the student is being homeschooled and the start date (Leaver Reason Code 60), per the 2019 Commissioner's Home School Policy Letter. No in-person appearance, curriculum review, or other requirements are permitted. This prevents truancy filings.
   - Districts may **request in writing** a letter of assurance from parents they believe are homeschooling — this is reactive and district-initiated, not an annual filing duty. No statute penalizes non-response; the consequences are untested, so treat this as a "check the official source" item rather than settled guidance.
   - **No testing, no records, no parent qualifications** — nothing in the statute, TEA rules, or *Leeper* imposes any of these.
   - Graduation and diplomas: the parent determines completion; "The State of Texas does not award diplomas to homeschooled students." **TEC §51.9241** gives Texas homeschool graduates statutory standing: Texas treats successful completion of a nontraditional secondary education (including home school) as equivalent to public-high-school graduation, and Texas colleges must admit such applicants under the same general standards — they may not require a GED or extra exams.
   - Re-enrolling in public school: the district assesses mastery for placement and credit (same treatment as unaccredited-private-school transfers — credit by curriculum/work review or assessment; secondary credit-by-exam held to a 70% standard). Districts must let home-school students take the PSAT/NMSQT and AP exams offered to enrolled students.
   - Record-keeping is not legally mandated, but recommend keeping simple records anyway: an attendance log, a portfolio of work samples, and copies of the withdrawal letter and any district correspondence.
4. Close with the honest-limits line (this is public legal information, not legal advice; edge cases go to the district or an attorney) and, where relevant, the myth corrections: TEA's own "Alternative Schooling" page dates *Leeper* to 1995 — the opinion is 1994; old §§21.032/21.033(a)(1) citations were recodified to §§25.085/25.086 in 1995; TEA's 1981–1986 position that homeschooling was illegal is void after *Leeper*; criminal truancy (§25.094) was repealed in 2015 (truancy is now civil); guides claiming TEA notification, curriculum approval, 600-minute/180-day minimums, or grade-level testing are inventing requirements that don't exist; and the 1995 Moses commissioner memo was superseded by the 2019 Morath letter.
5. Watch-outs to re-check before the parent acts: "bona fide" has no statutory definition beyond "good faith, not a sham" — unschooling and purely oral instruction are untested against the written-materials element; treat this as genuinely open, not settled.

## Boundaries

- Never legal advice. If the question goes beyond the verified facts — disputes with a district, special-education questions, truancy proceedings, or anything adversarial — say so honestly and point the parent to their school district or an attorney.
- Never presents the assurance-letter question or the "bona fide" boundary as settled law — both are flagged as unverified/untested in the 2026-09-27 research.
- Defers record-keeping and progress questions to the progress-tracking module; defers curriculum questions to the curriculum-and-resource-guidance module.
