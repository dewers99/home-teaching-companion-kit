# Pre-K developmental domains reference

**Version:** 1.0.0

*This appendix holds the developmental-domain structure the companion uses when planning for ages 3–5 (Pre-K / preschool). It is consulted when the parent asks for Pre-K planning, objectives, or activity ideas — never volunteered in full unprompted. Organized strictly by developmental domain, not by school subjects: that is how Pre-K works, and forcing subject labels onto this age distorts it. Milestones below are typical ranges, not deadlines — wide variation is normal and healthy. Domains below are typical for U.S. Pre-K planning, calibrated against Ohio's Early Learning and Development Standards as a worked example. This appendix is planning help, not legal requirements — the family's state compliance module is the binding document for what their state requires.*

## How the companion uses this appendix

- When the parent wants Pre-K plans, objectives, or activity ideas, work **domain by domain**, not subject by subject.
- **Kindergarten readiness** is the north star — a direction, not a test. If a 4-year-old isn't tracing letters yet, that is information, not failure.
- Sessions are short: **10–15 minutes** of focused time at age 3, stretching a bit by age 5. Play is the main vehicle; worksheets are the short seat-work slice, never the whole program.
- **Weekly themes** (apples, weather, community helpers, seasons…) are the typical organizing unit — one theme can carry activities across several domains at once.
- If the family chose faith integration at intake, a weekly Bible story and memory verse slot naturally into read-aloud time and the "my world" domain. If they didn't, leave it out entirely — never smuggle it in.

## Domain 1 — Early literacy & language

**What it is:** the foundations of reading and communicating: vocabulary, listening, letter knowledge, sounds, print awareness, and pre-writing.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Recognizes own name in print; later writes it
- Knows most uppercase letters, then lowercase; connects letters to their sounds
- Hears rhymes and beginning sounds; claps syllables
- Follows a story read aloud; retells it in own words; answers "what happened" questions
- Holds a crayon/pencil with a developing grip; traces lines, shapes, then letters
- Dictates stories for the parent to write down

**What it looks like at home:** daily read-aloud (20 minutes matters more than any worksheet), letter-of-the-week play, rhyming games, tracing in sand or shaving cream before pencil work.

**Practice-page layer:** a sequenced worksheet collection works well here in this order — *letters → letter sounds/phonics → words/vocabulary → simple reading comprehension* — with sight-word flashcards (Dolch pre-primer/primer) as an optional extra. Keep it to a few pages a week.

## Domain 2 — Early math & logic

**What it is:** number sense before arithmetic: counting with meaning, comparing, sorting, patterns, shapes, and spatial words.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Counts to 10 with one-to-one correspondence (touching each object), then to 20
- Recognizes and writes numerals 1–10
- Compares: more/less, bigger/smaller, longer/shorter
- Sorts by color, shape, or size; continues simple patterns (red-blue-red-blue)
- Names basic shapes (circle, square, triangle, rectangle) and finds them in the room
- Uses positional words: above, below, beside, between, in front, behind

**What it looks like at home:** counting snacks, sorting laundry by color, patterning with blocks, measuring with hands and cups. Concrete objects first — the abstract idea of "five" arrives on its own.

**Practice-page layer:** number tracing and sequencing sheets, shape and color pages, then simple picture-based addition/subtraction within 5 for the older end of the range.

## Domain 3 — Science & nature

**What it is:** observing the world and asking why: the five senses, living vs. nonliving, weather, seasons, plants, animals, and simple cause-and-effect.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Names the five senses and what each does
- Distinguishes living from nonliving things
- Observes and describes weather and seasonal change
- Plants a seed and watches it grow; knows plants need water and light
- Makes simple predictions ("will it sink or float?") and observes what happens

**What it looks like at home:** nature walks, weather wheel on the fridge, sink-or-float at the sink, growing beans in a cup, watching clouds. This domain lives outdoors more than at the table.

**Practice-page layer:** weather wheels, matching animals to homes, living/nonliving sorts — a few pages a month is plenty; the real work is outside.

## Domain 4 — My world (self, family, community)

**What it is:** the Pre-K roots of social studies and history: identity, family, community helpers, rules, and the calendar.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Knows own full name, age, and family members' names
- Names community helpers (firefighter, nurse, mail carrier) and what they do
- Understands rules keep people safe — at home and in the community
- Follows the calendar: days of the week, months, seasons, "yesterday/today/tomorrow"
- Takes part in family traditions and can talk about them

**What it looks like at home:** calendar time each morning, talking about the mail carrier, visiting the fire station, drawing the family, celebrating the seasons as they change.

## Domain 5 — Physical development

**What it is:** fine motor (hands) and gross motor (whole body) growth — the physical foundation everything academic sits on.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Fine: cuts with scissors along a line, traces and copies shapes/letters, strings beads, works playdough, buttons and zips
- Gross: runs, jumps, hops, gallops, throws/catches/kicks a ball, pedals or balances on a bike
- Builds hand strength and pencil control through play before formal writing

**What it looks like at home:** playground time, cutting practice, playdough challenges, ball games, drawing. If writing is hard, the answer is usually more hand-strengthening play, not more worksheets.

## Domain 6 — Creative arts

**What it is:** art, music, and movement as learning — not decoration on top of learning.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Explores art materials freely (paint, crayons, collage, clay) — process over product
- Sings songs, keeps a beat, moves to music
- Engages in pretend play and storytelling with props
- Draws recognizable people and scenes by age 4–5

**What it looks like at home:** a box of dress-up clothes, daily songs, an art shelf the child can reach, dancing in the kitchen. Don't correct the purple sun.

## Domain 7 — Social-emotional growth

**What it is:** naming feelings, self-control, friendship, and manners — the domain that makes every other domain possible.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Names basic feelings in self and others (happy, sad, angry, scared)
- Takes turns and shares — with reminders
- Follows simple two-step directions
- Shows growing self-control: waits briefly, uses words instead of hitting
- Plays cooperatively alongside and then with other children

**What it looks like at home:** naming feelings out loud ("you look frustrated"), emotion faces, storybooks about feelings, practicing apologies, consistent routines.

**Practice-page layer:** emotion-matching and coloring faces, "circle the feeling" pages — useful as conversation starters, not as tests.

## Domain 8 — Practical life skills

**What it is:** the everyday competencies that build independence and confidence: routines, self-care, helping, and healthy habits.

**Typical objectives (ages 3–5):**
- Dresses self (with help for tricky fasteners), washes hands independently
- Follows the daily routine with decreasing reminders
- Helps with simple household jobs: wiping the table, feeding a pet, sorting laundry
- Practices healthy habits: teeth, sleep routine, trying foods
- Cleans up after activities — with the parent modeling it first

**What it looks like at home:** a picture schedule on the wall, a step stool at the sink, real jobs that matter. Montessori-inspired practical tasks fit here naturally.

## Worked example: how this maps to Ohio's Early Learning and Development Standards

*Other states, countries, and territories publish their own early-learning standards with different domain names — the mapping logic is the same: find your area's domains, line them up against the eight below.*

Ohio's Early Learning and Development Standards (ELDS, revised June 2022, birth to kindergarten entry) name nine domains: Approaches to Learning, Cognitive Development, Creative Development, Language and Literacy, Mathematics, Physical Development and Wellness, Science, Social and Emotional Development, and Social Studies. This appendix's eight domains map onto seven of them directly (early literacy & language → Language and Literacy; early math & logic → Mathematics; science & nature → Science; my world → Social Studies; physical development → Physical Development and Wellness; creative arts → Creative Development; social-emotional growth → Social and Emotional Development; cognitive work is woven through all of them). Two deliberate differences: **practical life skills** is not an ELDS domain — it's a home-planning addition (routines, self-care, real jobs) because independence is half the Pre-K job at home. And **Approaches to Learning** — curiosity, initiative, persistence, inventiveness — isn't a separate domain here because it underlies every domain: a child who keeps trying is learning in all of them at once.

## Sample weekly rhythm

- **Morning time (5–15 min):** calendar, weather, a song, one question to wonder about
- **Play (the bulk):** free play plus one guided activity tied to the week's theme
- **Read-aloud (20 min):** can be bedtime; counts fully
- **Focused work (10–15 min):** one worksheet or hands-on task from a domain — letters one day, numbers the next
- **Outdoors daily:** nature, gross motor, and science all live here

## Kindergarten-readiness checklist

Not a test — a direction. By around age 5, most children are moving toward:
- Recognizing and writing their name; knowing most letter names and sounds
- Counting to 20; recognizing numerals 1–10
- Holding scissors and crayons with control; tracing and copying
- Listening to a story and retelling it; speaking in full sentences
- Taking turns, following two-step directions, managing feelings with support
- Dressing self and handling bathroom independently

A child missing pieces of this at 5 is not behind — it tells the parent where to lean in during the kindergarten year.

## Honest limits

This appendix describes typical developmental patterns for planning purposes. It is not a medical, psychological, or educational assessment tool. Children develop on wildly different timelines; if the parent has genuine concerns about a child's development, the companion should encourage talking to the child's pediatrician — never diagnose, label, or reassure away a concern.
