# 9–12 subject reference (9th through 12th grade)

**Version:** 1.0.0

*This appendix holds the typical subject structure and graduation planning guidance for 9th through 12th grade (roughly ages 14–18). Consulted when the parent asks what to teach, how credits work, or how to plan for graduation — never volunteered in full unprompted. This band is about planning backward from the student's goals, not checking boxes by grade. Content below is typical U.S. scope and sequence, calibrated against Ohio's learning standards as a worked example. It is planning help, not legal requirements — the family's state compliance module lists what their state actually requires (including any state-specific graduation or dual-enrollment rules), and that's the binding document.*

## How the companion uses this appendix

- Start from the student's **post-graduation direction**: four-year college, community college, trade/apprenticeship, military, workforce, gap year — then plan the four years backward from what that path expects.
- **Credits, not grade levels, are the currency.** One credit ≈ a full year of a subject (≈120–180 hours); a half credit ≈ a semester. Credit is based on the course's level, not the student's age — Algebra I or Biology completed in 8th grade counts on the high school transcript.
- A rough weekly rhythm: **20–25 focused hours per week** (4–5 hours/day), shifting toward the student's independent management. By 11th–12th grade the student should largely run their own schedule with the parent as advisor.
- The parent is the **guidance counselor**: track credits, build the transcript, research admissions or apprenticeship requirements, and sign the diploma.
- If the family chose faith integration at intake, theology/worldview study, apologetics, and service fit naturally as electives or core — still opt-in, still shaped by the family's choice.

## The typical credit framework

Most homeschool families aim for **22–28 total credits**. A common college-bound framework:

| Area | Typical credits |
|---|---|
| English (composition & literature) | 4 |
| Mathematics | 3–4 |
| Science (with labs) | 3–4 |
| Social studies | 3–4 |
| World language | 2–3 |
| Fine arts | 1 |
| Physical education / health | 1–2 |
| Technology | 1 |
| Personal finance / economics | 0.5–1 |
| Electives / career exploration | 3–6 |

Non-college paths adjust freely: a trade-bound student might swap the 4th math credit for shop, welding, or an apprenticeship — the framework serves the student, not the reverse.

## English (4 credits)

- **9th:** composition fundamentals + world literature (or ancient literature)
- **10th:** essays and research writing + classic/world literature
- **11th:** American literature + rhetoric and longer research papers
- **12th:** British literature + advanced composition; a senior thesis or capstone paper for the ambitious
- Throughout: vocabulary, grammar applied through editing (not isolated drills), and discussion — talking about books is the engine

## Mathematics (3–4 credits)

The standard college-bound sequence: **Algebra I → Geometry → Algebra II → Pre-Calculus** (→ Calculus for STEM-bound students).

- **Algebra I** (often 8th or 9th): equations, inequalities, functions introduced, factoring, quadratics
- **Geometry** (9th or 10th): proofs (even informal ones teach reasoning), constructions, coordinate geometry
- **Algebra II** (10th or 11th): advanced functions, logarithms, conic sections, sequences
- **Pre-Calculus / Trigonometry** (11th or 12th): the bridge to calculus
- Alternatives with dignity: consumer math, business math, statistics — a student headed for the trades or arts does not need calculus, and saying so honestly is a service

## Science (3–4 credits, with labs)

The standard sequence: **Biology → Chemistry → Physics**, plus electives.

- **Biology** (9th or 10th): cells, genetics, evolution (taught per the family's convictions — the companion follows the family's lead), ecology; microscope work
- **Chemistry** (10th or 11th): the math-heaviest science — best after Algebra I; real lab work with proper safety
- **Physics** (11th or 12th): conceptual or algebra-based; pairs well with pre-calculus
- Electives: anatomy & physiology, environmental science, astronomy, marine biology
- **Lab notebooks matter**: dated entries, procedures, data, conclusions — colleges may ask for lab documentation

## Social studies (3–4 credits)

- **World history / geography** (9th or 10th)
- **U.S. history** (10th or 11th): a full survey if 8th grade didn't cover it
- **U.S. government** (0.5–1 credit, often 12th): the Constitution in depth, civic participation — consider having the student follow a real election cycle
- **Economics / personal finance** (0.5–1 credit): budgeting, credit, investing basics, how the economy works — among the highest-real-world-value credits available

## World language (2–3 credits)

- Consistency beats intensity: a little daily over years
- Two years minimum for most four-year colleges; the language itself is the family's choice
- Libraries, apps, co-ops, and native-speaker conversation partners all count

## Electives & career exploration

- **Fine arts** (1 credit): music, visual art, drama, photography
- **Technology** (1 credit): coding, digital media, computer applications
- **Career pathways**: job shadowing, internships, apprenticeships, trade coursework, certifications — documented like any other credit
- **Dual enrollment**: community college courses — typically 11th–12th grade — earn high school and college credit simultaneously, often the highest-value move available. Example (Ohio): College Credit Plus is open to homeschool students in **grades 7–12**: if awarded state funding, tuition at a public college is free (families buy textbooks). Other states run their own dual-enrollment programs with their own rules — check the family's state module. Check local community college policies and the state education department's dual-enrollment page for current dates and funding.
- **AP / CLEP**: for the college-bound; CLEP exams can earn real college credit by examination

## The transcript & diploma

- Before building anything, check the family's state compliance module for state-specific rules, recommendations, or requirements on transcripts, diplomas, graduation documentation, or scholarship paperwork — build to satisfy those first, then the general guidance below.
- The parent builds the **transcript**: student name and birthdate, school name, year-by-year courses with credits, grades, and GPA, grading scale, test scores (SAT/ACT if taken), and a legend explaining any honors/AP/dual-enrollment designations.
- The parent issues the **diploma** — the family sets its own graduation requirements and credentials. (Not legal advice; the state compliance module carries the details.)
- Keep **course descriptions** (a paragraph per course: materials used, topics covered) — some colleges ask for them.
- Start the transcript in 9th grade and update it yearly; reconstructing four years from memory in 12th grade is miserable.

## Testing & admissions (college-bound)

- **PSAT** (10th–11th), **SAT/ACT** (11th): most colleges still accept these; test-optional policies vary — check each school
- Admissions offices evaluate homeschool transcripts routinely; the transcript, course descriptions, test scores, essays, and recommendations (from co-op teachers, employers, mentors — anyone but the parent) tell the story
- Begin researching target schools' homeschool admissions pages in **10th grade**

## Paths that aren't four-year college

- **Community college**: open admissions, transferable credits, career programs — an excellent default
- **Trades & apprenticeships**: electrician, plumbing, HVAC, welding, automotive — paid training, no debt; document the training as career-pathway credits
- **Military**: homeschool graduates enlist; recruiters know the drill
- **Workforce / entrepreneurship**: the family business, freelancing, certifications (IT, real estate, etc.)
- **Gap year**: work, service, travel — with a plan, not a drift
- None of these is a consolation prize. The companion treats the student's actual goals as the plan.

## Honest limits

This appendix reflects widely used homeschool graduation patterns and college admissions practices — not legal advice, not a certified curriculum, and not a guarantee of admission anywhere. Credit frameworks vary between families and states; admissions requirements vary between schools. When the parent asks about legal graduation requirements, defer to the compliance module and the actual statute. When they ask what a specific college expects, the answer is always "check that college's admissions page."
