# 6–8 subject reference (6th through 8th grade)

**Version:** 1.0.1

*This appendix holds the typical subject structure and grade-level objectives for 6th through 8th grade (roughly ages 11–14). Consulted when the parent asks what to teach, in what order, or whether their child is on track — never volunteered in full unprompted. Objectives are typical ranges, not deadlines; children routinely work across grade levels in different subjects. Content below is typical U.S. scope and sequence, calibrated against Ohio's learning standards as a worked example. It is planning help, not legal requirements — the family's state compliance module lists what their state actually requires, and that's the binding document.*

## How the companion uses this appendix

- Work **subject by subject**, grade by grade. Start from what the student can do today, not the grade label.
- The big shift in 6–8: the student becomes a **self-managing learner**. The parent moves from teacher toward coach — teaching the student to plan a week, track assignments, study for a test, and ask for help.
- A rough weekly rhythm: **18–22 focused hours per week** (about 4–4.5 hours/day over 5 days). A common split: math ~5 hrs, language arts ~5–6 hrs, science ~3–4 hrs, social studies ~3–4 hrs, arts/PE/electives ~2–3 hrs, plus ~30 min/day independent reading. Homeschools finish faster than the classroom clock — adjust to the child, not a bell schedule.
- **Record-keeping starts to matter here.** Encourage the parent to keep reading lists, course descriptions, major projects, science labs, and any high-school-level coursework — this becomes the high school transcript's foundation. (The progress-tracking module handles the how.)
- If the family chose faith integration at intake, independent Bible study, discussion, and worldview conversations fit naturally at this age — still opt-in, still shaped by the family's choice.

## English language arts

**6th grade**
- Reads full-length novels with attention to plot, character, setting, and theme
- Writes structured paragraphs and short essays with evidence from the text
- Grammar: parts of speech solid; phrases and clauses introduced; consistent mechanics
- Vocabulary: Latin and Greek roots begin paying dividends

**7th grade**
- Literary analysis deepens: symbolism, point of view, comparing texts
- Research skills: finding sources, evaluating credibility, citing, avoiding plagiarism
- Essays grow longer and more organized; creative writing continues
- Public speaking or debate as an optional stretch

**8th grade**
- High-school-ready writing: multi-paragraph essays with thesis statements, introductions, conclusions
- Reads across genres including Shakespeare excerpts, classic short stories, foundational documents
- Grammar and mechanics should be largely automatic; editing others' work sharpens it
- A research paper (with real citations) is the capstone writing project for many families

## Mathematics

The standard path is **6th → 7th → Pre-Algebra → Algebra I**, but pacing varies enormously — an 8th grader in Algebra I and an 8th grader finishing pre-algebra are both fine.

**6th grade**
- Ratios, rates, and unit rates; percent as a rate per 100
- Division of fractions; fluency with multi-digit decimals
- Negative numbers introduced; expressions and one-step equations
- Area of triangles and polygons; volume of rectangular prisms; statistical thinking (mean, median, data displays)

**7th grade**
- Operations with positive and negative rational numbers (integers, fractions, decimals)
- Proportional relationships: unit rate, constant of proportionality in tables, graphs, equations
- Real-world percent problems: tax, tip, discount, markup, simple interest
- Two-step equations and inequalities; scale drawings; circles (circumference and area); angle relationships; probability and sampling

**8th grade / Pre-Algebra**
- Linear equations in one and two variables; slope; graphing lines
- Functions introduced (input/output, not formal notation yet)
- Systems of equations (intuitive/graphical); the Pythagorean theorem
- Exponents and scientific notation; transformations (rotations, reflections, translations)
- **Algebra I** (often 8th or 9th): solving multi-step equations, factoring, quadratics introduced, graphing functions

## Science

Two common patterns — pick the one the family prefers; both are legitimate:
- **Discipline-per-year rotation:** earth science → life science → physical science (any order)
- **Integrated:** topics from all three branches each year, deepening annually

- **Earth/space:** plate tectonics, rock cycle, weather and climate systems, the solar system and universe
- **Life:** cells, genetics and heredity basics, ecosystems, human body systems in more depth
- **Physical:** matter and chemical reactions, forces and motion, energy transformations, waves
- Throughout: **real lab work** — hypothesis, procedure, data tables, conclusion — recorded in a lab notebook. Labs are what separate middle-school science from elementary science.

## History, government & social studies

The most common arc (families and states vary — any coherent arc works):

- **6th — Ancient world:** early civilizations through Rome; geography of the ancient world; how historians know what they know (primary vs. secondary sources)
- **7th — Medieval to early modern:** Middle Ages, Renaissance, Reformation, age of exploration; world geography and cultures
- **8th — U.S. history:** a full survey from colonization through the modern era, or the second half if 5th covered the first — many families do Civics/government in depth here
- **Government strand:** the Constitution as a document (not just a story), the Bill of Rights, federalism, the branches in action, civic participation — an 8th-grade civics deep-dive serves the student for life
- Primary sources enter here: excerpts from real documents, speeches, letters — short, guided, discussed

## Electives (begin in the middle grades)

- **Foreign language:** the most common first elective; consistency beats intensity (a little daily)
- **Logic / critical thinking:** a natural fit for this age and a quiet superpower for high school
- **Fine arts:** instrument lessons, choir, drawing/painting instruction, drama
- **Technology:** keyboarding fluency, basic coding, digital media skills
- **Health & PE:** regular physical activity (~60 min most days); general wellness and body literacy — nutrition, sleep, hygiene, and the normal changes of adolescence

## Study & independence skills (explicitly taught)

- Running a planner: writing down assignments, working backward from deadlines
- Note-taking from lectures and reading (Cornell notes, outlining, mind-mapping — try several)
- Test preparation: spaced review, self-quizzing, managing test anxiety
- Asking for help: identifying what's confusing and articulating the question
- Managing a multi-day project: breaking it into steps with mini-deadlines

## Honest limits

This appendix reflects widely used homeschool scope-and-sequence patterns and public academic standards — not a certified curriculum and not an assessment tool. Grade labels are conveniences. History arcs and science rotations vary legitimately between families and states. If the parent asks about alignment to a specific state standard, only claim what can honestly be checked against the actual standard.
